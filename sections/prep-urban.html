Urban & Flats

A city flat has less space, no garden and a lot of neighbours, but it also has thick walls, people close by and shops a short walk away. City prepping is about fitting stores into small spaces without overloading the floor, knowing what fails first in a tall building, and being able to get home on foot when the trains stop.

1. What’s Different in a Flat

In a flat or tall buildingWhat it means
Lifts stop in a power cutEveryone uses the stairs, including anyone who can’t easily. Store water, not just food: carrying it up 10 floors is hard.
Water is often pumped upMany taller blocks use electric booster pumps. In a power cut the top floors lose water first, sometimes within hours. Store more water the higher you live.
Little storage, upstairs floorsStore in small, spread-out amounts and check the floor load (§3).
Shared doors, entry systems, corridorsDoor entry may fail open or locked. Know how to get out manually and where the fire exits go.
No garden, no fireNo barbecue, no bucket-toilet emptying outdoors. Cook on a camping stove by an open window only; plan sanitation with bags and lidded bins.
Lots of neighboursMore help, more skills, more people who need checking on. Also: more people who know what you have.

2. Storing in Small Spaces

  • Under beds: 2 L bottles lie flat; shallow crates of tins on wheels. Raise the bed on risers for more room.
  • Kitchen plinths: the kick-board under kitchen units usually pops off and hides a 10 cm space the length of the kitchen, perfect for tins and bottles.
  • Wardrobe floors, behind the sofa, the bottom of every cupboard: heavy things low.
  • Back of doors: shoe organisers hold batteries, torches, first aid, tins of fish.
  • The bath: if a water outage is forecast, fill it, or use a bath water bladder that keeps it clean enough to drink.
  • Balconies: fine for a locked box of non-food kit. Not for water, food or batteries: sun and frost ruin them.
  • Copy-cupboard: keep your normal cupboards a few weeks deeper rather than a separate “prepper” store. It rotates itself and nobody notices.

3. Water Is Heavy: Floor Loads

A litre of water weighs a kilogram. UK homes are designed for an everyday load of about 1.5 kN/m² spread across the floor (roughly 150 kg on each square metre) and about 200 kg on one small spot. A row of jerrycans is fine. A 200-litre barrel in the middle of a bedroom upstairs is not.

This is a rule of thumb, not a structural survey. If you want to store hundreds of litres upstairs, or have an older building with sagging floors, ask a structural engineer. Leaks matter too in a flat: keep containers in a tray, and never in a loft above someone’s ceiling.

4. High-Rise Living

  • Walk both stair routes now, in daylight, to the ground and out. Count the floors. Find where each exit comes out.
  • Read your building’s fire action notice. Some blocks have a “stay put” policy (your flat is built to hold a fire back), others tell everyone to leave. Never use the lift in a fire. If you are told to leave, or smoke reaches your flat, go.
  • If you can’t manage stairs, ask your landlord or building manager for a personal emergency evacuation plan (PEEP), and tell your neighbours.
  • Keep a torch by the front door: stairwell emergency lights last only a few hours.
  • Water: fill containers as soon as the power goes, before the pumps’ tanks run down.
  • Heat rises: top flats stay warmer in a winter power cut and overheat in summer. Ground-floor flats flood first.

5. Staying Put or Leaving the City

In almost every urban emergency, stay put. You have shelter, stores and neighbours; roads out will be jammed; and the authorities plan around people staying home. Leave when:

  • Told to evacuate by the police, fire service or council
  • The building is on fire, structurally damaged, or has a gas leak
  • Flood water is coming in and you can’t get higher
  • No water, power or heat for so long that staying is more dangerous than going (a winter outage, with older people)
  • Violence or unrest has reached your street

If you leave, go early, before the roads fill, to somewhere you’ve arranged in advance (family, friends: your “out of area” meeting point). Decision help: the Should You Stay or Go? tool, and Bug-Out vs Bug-In.

6. The Get-Home Bag and the Walk Home

Most people are away from home in the daytime. If the trains stop, the roads jam and the phone network is down, you may have to walk home, perhaps 10, 20 or 40 km. A get-home bag lives under your desk or in the car, and is light enough to walk all day with.

Packing it, layer by layer. Heavy things in the middle and close to your back; what you need fast on top.

  • Comfortable walking shoes and thick socks (if you work in smart shoes)
  • Water: 1 L bottle and a filter bottle
  • Snacks for a day: nuts, bars, chocolate
  • Waterproof jacket, warm hat, gloves, foil blanket
  • Head torch
  • Power bank and cable
  • Paper map of the route home, with your second route marked
  • Cash in small notes
  • Basic first aid, blister plasters, any medicines
  • Face mask for dust or smoke
  • Small notebook, pen, list of numbers

Planning the route

  • Walk with colleagues going your way. Tell family your route and stick to it, so they know where to look.
  • Leave early, in daylight. Most people wait to see what happens.
  • Agree with family: if networks are down, everyone heads home (or to the meeting point) and waits there. Don’t go looking for each other.
  • Children at school: schools keep children until a named adult collects them. Agree who that is.

7. Keeping a Low Profile

In a long crisis, the flat with lights on when the street is dark, or the person carrying in a trolley of water, gets noticed. You don’t need to hide, just don’t advertise:

  • Black-out curtains or a blanket on the window when you use lights at night.
  • Bring stores in a little at a time, in ordinary shopping bags. Keep cooking smells down where you can.
  • Don’t post your preps online, and keep the details within the household.
  • Share generously, but in a way that doesn’t show what’s left. More: Operational Security.

8. Organise Your Building

A block of flats is a ready-made village. In a long power cut, the buildings that coped were the ones where neighbours already knew each other.

  • Start a residents’ group chat now, and a paper list of flat numbers, names and phone numbers for when the network is down.
  • Know who lives alone, who is elderly, disabled, or has a baby, and who needs power for medical equipment.
  • List skills: nurses, electricians, plumbers, people with first aid or radio licences.
  • Agree a meeting place (the lobby, a ground-floor flat) and a notice board for news.
  • Pool what makes sense: one big water filter, one camping stove kettle for hot drinks, one radio for news.
