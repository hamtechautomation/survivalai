Stockpile & Rotation

A store of food is only useful if it is the right amount, of things you will actually eat, still good when you need it. This page works out how much your household needs, what to put in it, how long each thing keeps, how to seal dry food for decades, and how to rotate it so nothing is wasted.

2. What to Store

A store should feed you properly, not just fill you up. Build it in five groups, mostly from things you already buy:

GroupShareGood choices
Starches (energy)About halfRice, pasta, oats, flour, couscous, crackers, instant mash, tinned potatoes
ProteinA fifthTinned fish and meat, beans, lentils, chickpeas, peanut butter, nuts, powdered milk, protein-rich soups
Fats and oilsA little, but vitalVegetable and olive oil, ghee, tinned fish in oil. Fat is the densest energy you can store.
Fruit and vegetablesA fifthTinned tomatoes, vegetables and fruit, dried fruit, fruit juice, soups. Add a multivitamin for a long store.
Flavour and comfortSmall but importantSalt, sugar, honey, stock cubes, spices, sauces, tea, coffee, chocolate, biscuits. Bland food is hard to eat for weeks.
  • Store what you eat, eat what you store. If nobody in the house likes lentils, don’t buy 10 kg of them.
  • Food that needs no cooking matters most in the first days, when fuel, water and time are short.
  • Special diets: baby food and formula, gluten-free, diabetic, allergies. Store more of these, as they are the first to vanish from shops.
  • Pets eat too: two weeks of food at least.

3. How Long Things Keep

Stored cool (below about 20 °C), dark and dry. Heat is the enemy: as a rule of thumb, shelf life roughly halves for each 10 °C warmer. A garage that reaches 30 °C in summer or a loft is a poor store.

Sealed figures follow Brigham Young University’s 2024 food-storage guide, the main long-term study (an earlier 2008 version gave 30 years for several of these; the newer figures are more cautious). They assume airtight, low-oxygen packaging kept cool, and mean still acceptable to eat, not as good as new.

4. Best Before vs Use By

Use by: safety

On fresh meat, fish, dairy and ready meals. Don’t eat after the date, even if it looks and smells fine: the bacteria that make you ill don’t always show.

Best before: quality

On tins, dried food, frozen food. Still safe after the date if stored properly and the pack is undamaged; it may taste less fresh. Most of a store is “best before”.

⚠️
Throw out any tin that is

Bulging or domed, leaking, deeply dented (especially on a seam or rim), badly rusted, or that spurts when opened, smells off or looks cloudy when it shouldn’t. These can carry botulism, which can kill. Don’t taste to check.

5. Where and How to Store

  • Cool, dark, dry, and off the floor: under stairs, the back of cupboards, under beds. Not the loft, not a hot garage, not by the boiler.
  • Pests: mice chew through bags, and pantry moths get into flour and oats. Decant opened dry food into jars or tubs with tight lids. Buckets with lids keep mice out of bulk stores.
  • Spread it out: some in the kitchen, some under the stairs, some in a grab bag. A flood or fire won’t take it all.
  • Keep a list of what you have and where (the tracker in §7), so you can find it in the dark.

6. Sealing Dry Food for Years

Oxygen and moisture make food go stale and let insects survive. Sealing dry food in Mylar (foil) bags with oxygen absorbers removes both, and gives the decades-long figures above.

  1. Only dry, low-fat foods: white rice, beans, oats, pasta, wheat, sugar (without absorber), powdered milk. Moisture below about 10%: if it’s soft, sticky or oily, it can’t go in.
  2. Food-grade Mylar bags (thick, 5 mil or more) and oxygen absorbers sized to the bag: roughly 300–500 cc for a 4-litre bag of grain, about 2,000 cc for a 20-litre bucket. Pasta and other airy foods need more.
  3. Fill, shake down, add the absorber last. Absorbers start working as soon as they meet air: open the pack, work quickly, and reseal the leftovers in a jar at once.
  4. Press out the air and seal with a hot iron, hair straighteners or an impulse sealer. Leave a little headroom so you can open and reseal it.
  5. Label it with contents and date. Within a day or two the bag should pull tight and hard as the oxygen goes.
  6. Store the bags in buckets: Mylar stops air and light, not mice.
⚠️
Never seal moist food with oxygen absorbers

Botulism bacteria grow without oxygen. In a sealed bag of anything moist (fresh food, home-dried fruit that isn’t brittle-dry, cooked food), removing the oxygen helps them. Moist food is preserved by canning, freezing, salting or drying properly instead: Food Preservation.

7. Rotation: Oldest First

Rotation means you eat from your store all the time and replace what you eat, so nothing gets old. New stock goes in at the back; you take from the front.

8. Not Just Food

Shortages hit everyday things as hard as food. Keep a few weeks of these, and rotate them the same way:

  • Toilet roll, kitchen roll, bin bags (heavy-duty)
  • Soap, hand sanitiser, shampoo, toothpaste, toothbrushes
  • Sanitary products, nappies and wipes
  • Plain unscented bleach (it weakens with age: replace it every year)
  • Washing-up liquid, laundry soap
  • Painkillers, rehydration sachets, antihistamines, your usual medicines
  • Batteries (lithium AA and AAA keep longest), lighters, matches
  • Camping gas cartridges or bottles
  • Pet food and cat litter
  • Seeds for the garden or windowsill

Water stores rotate too: tap water in your own containers every 6 months, bottled water by its date. See The Prepped Home: Water.
