Campervan & Mobile

A campervan is a small house that can leave: bed, kitchen, water, power and heat that go where you go. That makes it a superb emergency shelter and the best bug-out vehicle most people will own. It also packs gas, batteries and a heater into a sealed box, so it needs care. This page covers how long your van lasts parked, how to keep it safe, and how to use it in a crisis.

1. The Van as a Prep

Strengths

  • Shelter, bed and kitchen in one, ready in minutes
  • Leaves early with everything already packed
  • Its own power (solar, battery) and water
  • A warm, dry place to sleep if the house is damaged or flooded
  • Somewhere for relatives to stay, on your drive

Weaknesses

  • Needs fuel, and roads that are open
  • Small: water and battery last days, not weeks
  • Cold in winter without a heater, hot in summer
  • Gas, batteries and heaters in a small sealed space
  • Easy to spot, easy to steal from

3. Choosing a Van for Prepping

Most UK campervans are built on a handful of base vans (Ford Transit, the Fiat Ducato family that includes the Citroën Relay and Peugeot Boxer, the VW Transporter, the Mercedes Sprinter, the Renault Trafic and Master). Common vans mean common parts: any garage, and most scrapyards, can fix them.

ChoiceFor prepping
Panel-van conversion vs coachbuilt motorhomeA panel van is stronger, more discreet and easier to drive and park. A coachbuilt has more room and storage but a lighter body that is easier to damage.
SizeA short or medium van fits normal parking spaces and narrow lanes. A long van carries a fixed bed, more water and more storage.
Weight and licenceStay at or under 3,500 kg maximum authorised mass if anyone who passed their test after 1 January 1997 will drive (§4).
Diesel and AdBlueMost vans are diesel. Euro 6 diesels need AdBlue: if it runs out, the engine won’t restart. Carry a 10 L bottle. Check clean air zone rules for older diesels.
4×4 vs tyresTrue 4×4 vans are rare and costly. All-terrain or all-season tyres, good ground clearance and traction mats get most people through snow and muddy fields.
Insulation and heatingWorth more than any gadget for winter use. A diesel heater and insulated walls, floor and roof make a van liveable in a UK winter.
InsuranceTell your insurer about any conversion. To be insured as a motor caravan the conversion usually has to meet the DVLA’s description of one (bed, cooking, seating, storage, windows).

4. Weight, Payload and Your Licence

Vans are easy to overload: water, gas, batteries, people and a few weeks of food add up fast. A category B car licence covers vehicles up to 3,500 kg maximum authorised mass (MAM). Drivers who passed before 1 January 1997 usually also hold C1, up to 7,500 kg: check the back of your licence. What matters is the van’s rated MAM, not what it weighs on the day; carrying more than the rating is overloading, a separate offence.

  • Weigh it loaded at a public weighbridge (councils and recycling sites have them) before a long trip, including each axle.
  • Travel with less water and fill up where you stop: 70 litres is 70 kg.
  • Heavy things low and between the axles: water, batteries, tools. Bike racks and roof boxes on the back overload the rear axle quickly.

5. How Long Can You Last Parked?

Three things decide how long a van can stay put: the battery, the fresh water tank and the grey tank. Whichever runs out first sets the limit. Most vans manage two or three days on their own; with good solar in summer and careful water use, a week or more.

  • Lead-acid and AGM batteries shouldn’t go below half charge; only half the rated amp-hours are usable. Lithium (LiFePO4) gives about 90%, weighs half as much and lasts far longer, but must not be charged below 0 °C unless it has a built-in heater.
  • Solar in a UK winter gives perhaps a sixth of its summer output. A battery-to-battery (B2B) charger tops up from the engine while you drive: an hour’s drive with a 20 A charger puts back about 20 Ah.
  • Inverters are hungry. A hair dryer, kettle or microwave on an inverter flattens a 100 Ah battery in minutes. Boil water on gas.
  • Cutting water use: a pump-action spray bottle for washing up, wet wipes, a 5 L “shower” from a garden sprayer. Carry two extra 10–20 L water containers.

6. The 12 V System

Every van’s electrics follow the same pattern: three ways to charge one leisure battery, and a fuse box that feeds everything else. Understanding it lets you find most faults with a torch and a meter.

FaultCheck first
Nothing works in the backIsolator switch; main fuse by the battery; battery voltage (below about 11.8 V lead-acid is flat)
One thing doesn’t workIts fuse in the fuse box; then its switch and connections
Battery doesn’t charge while drivingB2B charger fuse and its ignition wire; engine battery voltage with the engine running (about 13.8–14.5 V)
Solar not chargingPanel dirty or shaded; controller fuses; connectors on the roof
Water pump runs on and offA dripping tap or a leak, or air in the system; the pump’s pressure switch

7. Gas, Heaters and Carbon Monoxide

⚠️
Carbon monoxide kills people in vans, tents and caravans every year

It has no smell. Early signs are headache, dizziness, nausea and sleepiness, the same as a hangover or the flu. Fit a CO alarm made for leisure vehicles (marked BS EN 50291-2), and test it weekly. If it sounds, or several people feel ill: turn off all burners, get everyone out into fresh air, and call 999 if anyone is unwell.

  • Never use a portable gas heater, a barbecue or a camping stove to heat a closed van. Cooking: open the roof vent or a window, and turn the hob off when you’ve finished.
  • Gas locker: sealed from the living space, with a drop-out vent in its floor. Bottles upright and strapped. Never store spare bottles inside the van.
  • Check for leaks with soapy water on joints: bubbles mean a leak. Smell gas? All gas off at the bottle, no switches or flames, open doors, get out.
  • Propane, not butane, in winter: butane won’t vaporise near freezing, so the gas stops. Use propane (red bottles from the main UK supplier) from autumn to spring.
  • Diesel heaters burn outside the living space and blow in clean warm air. Keep the exhaust clear of snow, hedges and other people’s windows, and service it yearly. One uses roughly 0.1–0.25 litres of diesel an hour.
  • Get a habitation check every year, including gas, electrics and damp, by a qualified engineer.

8. Water and Waste

  • Fill only from drinking-water taps, with your own food-grade hose. Sterilise the tank and pipes at the start of each season (sterilising fluid or a weak bleach solution, then rinse).
  • A water filter on the kitchen tap, or a filter bottle, lets you refill from doubtful sources in an emergency. Treating unknown water: Food & Water §2.
  • Frost: water in a pump, pipes or an outboard tank freezes and splits them. Run the heater, or drain everything before a hard frost.
  • Grey water (washing-up and shower) goes at a campsite or service point, never into a drain, a ditch or a stream.
  • Cassette toilets are emptied only at chemical toilet disposal points (campsites, many service areas). In a long emergency, use the bucket-toilet method from The Prepped Home, with bags.

9. Where You Can Stop in the UK

WhereRules
CampsitesWater, waste, power hook-up. Book ahead in season.
Small club sitesThe two big clubs both run members-only small sites (up to five vans) on farms and private land. Cheap, quiet, often with water.
Pubs, farms, private landOnly with the owner’s permission. Many pubs and farms welcome an overnight stop if you buy a meal.
Roadsides and lay-bysNo national law bans sleeping in a parked vehicle on a public road, but many councils and landowners restrict overnight parking (look for signs), most motorway services forbid it, and you can be asked to move on. Never park where you obstruct, and remember: asleep in the driver’s seat after a drink can still count as “in charge”.
Wild campingScotland’s right to roam is for people on foot and does not cover vehicles. In England and Wales, almost all land belongs to someone: ask.
In an emergencyFriends’ and family’s driveways, arranged in advance, are the best places to go. Agree it now, and put it in your plan.

Leave no trace: take rubbish with you, don’t empty anything on the ground, and keep the van looking like a visitor, not a camp.

10. Keeping the Van (and You) Safe

  • Theft: a steering-wheel lock and an OBD-port lock stop most thieves; a tracker helps get it back. Extra deadlocks on the side and back doors. Don’t leave kit on show.
  • Stores out of sight: food, water and fuel in lockers and under the floor, not on the seats. A van that looks empty is ignored.
  • Overnight: park facing the way out, keys always in the same place, the driver’s seat clear, so you can drive off without stepping outside. If someone threatens you in the night, leave first and ask questions later.
  • Privacy: blackout screens hide light and people inside. A van glowing at night draws attention.
  • Tell someone where you are each night, especially when travelling alone.

11. Living in the Van

NeedHow
FoodKeep the fridge full and shut; one-pot meals; a pressure cooker or a flask “haybox” saves gas. Lockers that latch, so tins don’t fly when you brake.
WashingA 5 L pump sprayer makes a shower; wet wipes and a flannel in between. Leisure centres, gyms, swimming pools and many truck stops have showers.
LaundryLaunderettes, campsites, or a bucket with a lid: a short drive agitates it like a washing machine.
ToiletCassette toilet emptied at disposal points; in a long emergency, a bucket with bags (how).
Heat in summerA parked van in the sun gets dangerously hot within minutes. Reflective screens, roof vent fan, park in shade, and never leave a dog or child inside.
PetsCrate or harness while driving, their own water, food and medicines, and a cool place to stay.
Living in it long-termDVLA, your insurer and your bank need an address: tell your insurer if you live in the van full-time. You can register with a GP without a fixed address.

12. Keeping the Van Half-Packed

A van that is already half-packed can leave in ten minutes. Keep these in it all the time, and rotate the food and water.

  • Fuel tank at least half full, always
  • Fresh water: tank part-filled, plus two 10–20 L containers
  • Three days of food that needs little cooking, and a manual tin opener
  • Spare gas bottle (propane in winter), lighter and matches
  • Bedding and warm clothes for everyone who might travel
  • First aid kit, any medicines, spare glasses
  • Head torches, a lantern, spare batteries
  • Radio, power bank, 12 V phone chargers
  • Toilet bags, toilet roll, hand wash, bin bags
  • Tools: jump leads, tyre inflator, spare fuses, tow rope, duct tape, cable ties
  • Shovel, snow chains or socks (winter), traction mats
  • Paper road atlas, with your routes out and meeting points marked
  • Cash in small notes, copies of documents
  • A 72-hour bag for each person you could walk with, if the van must be left

13. Spares and Weekly Checks

In a crisis, garages are shut. A van you look after, with a small box of spares, keeps going. Every week, and before every long trip, walk round it with FLOWER:

  • Fuel: tank at least half full; AdBlue topped up
  • Lights: all working, lenses clean
  • Oil: engine oil between the marks
  • Water: coolant and screenwash levels
  • Electrics: horn, warning lights, leisure battery voltage
  • Rubber: tyres at the loaded pressures, at least 1.6 mm tread, no cuts; wiper blades
  • Yourself: rested, and fit to drive
  • Fuses of every size used, bulbs, a 12 V test light or meter
  • Engine oil, coolant, AdBlue, screenwash
  • Fuel filter and the tool to change it (diesels)
  • Tyre repair kit, 12 V inflator, tyre pressure gauge, the locking-wheel-nut key
  • Lithium jump starter
  • Auxiliary belt, a couple of hose clips, self-amalgamating tape
  • Cable ties, duct tape, wire, a basic socket and spanner set
  • Gas washers, a spare regulator hose, soapy water spray for leak checks

14. Winter in the Van

  • Heat: a diesel heater is the best way to keep a van warm. Without one, rely on sleeping bags rated to −5 °C or lower, wool layers, hats, and hot water bottles.
  • Condensation: two people breathing and cooking put litres of water into the air overnight. Keep a vent open a crack, wipe windows in the morning, and air the bedding. Damp makes cold far worse.
  • Insulation: thermal screens on the cab windows make the biggest difference. A curtain between the cab and the living area helps.
  • Fuel: UK pumps sell a winter grade of diesel from about mid-November to mid-March, rated not to clog filters down to −15 °C. Old summer diesel can wax in a hard frost.
  • Batteries: lead-acid batteries lose capacity in the cold, and lithium won’t charge below 0 °C without a heater. Expect less from both.

15. The Van as a Bug-Out Vehicle

  1. Decide early. The roads clog fast. The van’s advantage is leaving before everyone else, not after.
  2. Know three routes to your destination, avoiding motorways and the city centre. Paper maps, as phone networks may be down.
  3. Fuel: stations need power to pump. Keep the tank half full and carry fuel only in approved cans, within the legal limits (rules).
  4. Travel together if there are two vehicles: same route, agreed stopping points, radios if you have them (Communications).
  5. Know when to leave the van: if the road is blocked, fuel runs out, or staying with the van becomes dangerous (flood water: 30 cm of moving water can float a car). Take the 72-hour bags and walk.

Van drills

  • Packed and away from home in under 30 minutes
  • Three days parked off-grid without hook-up
  • Changed a wheel (or used the repair kit) yourself
  • Found and replaced a blown fuse in the dark
  • Drove your three routes out, with a paper map
  • Drained the water system as if for a frost

16. A Winter Car Kit

Not everyone has a van, but almost everyone drives. Each winter, people are stranded overnight in snow on UK roads. Keep in every car:

  • Blanket or sleeping bag for each seat, and warm clothes
  • Water and snacks
  • Torch, phone charger and power bank
  • Ice scraper, de-icer, shovel, a bag of grit or sand
  • Hi-vis vest, warning triangle, first aid kit
  • Jump leads, tow rope
  • Boots, hat and gloves

Stuck in snow? Stay with the car unless help is close and visible. Run the engine for about ten minutes an hour for heat, with a window open a crack, and keep the exhaust clear of snow: a blocked exhaust fills the car with carbon monoxide.
