Prepping with Kids & Family

Prepping isn't about a bunker; it's about a family that knows what to do. Make it calm, practical, and even fun.

The essentials

Make a plan everyone understands. Who collects whom, where you meet if you can't get home, and who outside the area everyone calls. The government's household emergency plan template (prepare.campaign.gov.uk) takes 15 minutes. Teach every child old enough how to call 999, your address and postcode, and a parent's mobile number by heart. Practise, don't preach. Turn drills into games and camping trips. Calm adults raise calm children. Plan for the most vulnerable person first: babies, older relatives, anyone on medication or powered equipment. Sign up to the Priority Services Register.

1. The Family Emergency Plan

Fill this in together and keep a copy in every bag, the car and on the fridge:

  • Contacts: each adult's mobile; an out-of-area contact (an aunt in another county), because long-distance calls often get through when local networks jam; and the neighbours.
  • Meeting points:
    • ① just outside the home (fire);
    • ② a nearby landmark (if you can't get home);
    • ③ an out-of-town rally point (a relative's house) if your area is evacuated.
  • School and childcare: who's on the collection list (add a trusted neighbour), the school's emergency arrangements (ask the school), and what children should do if no one comes. The answer is always to stay with the teachers.
  • Medical: prescriptions, allergies, NHS numbers, GP, and who carries the spare inhaler or EpiPen.
  • Utilities: where the stopcock, gas valve and fuse box are, and who knows how to turn them off.
  • Pets: who takes them, and a pet-friendly fallback.
  • Local radio frequencies (see your regional guide).
  • Cash: small notes and coins for when card machines and cash points are down. The government's Be Ready campaign (Oct 2026) lists it alongside water, food, a battery radio, a first aid kit, medicines and a charged power bank.
🏠
Start with the government's three days.

The Be Ready checklist asks every household to hold three days of supplies, with 3 litres of water per person per day, and to check them every six months. Use the Stockpile Calculator to size it for your family, babies and pets included.

2. What to Teach Children, by Age

AgeSkills
3–5Their full name, a parent's name, and "999" for police, fire and ambulance. If lost: stand still, then ask a police officer, a shop worker, or a mum or dad with children. Wear a wristband or a card with your number on it in crowds.
6–8Address and postcode; a parent's mobile by heart. How to call 999 and what they'll be asked ("Which service? Where are you?"). The home fire escape plan. Packing their own grab bag. Using a whistle: six blasts, then a pause means "help" (the UK distress signal).
9–12Basic first aid (recovery position, pressure on bleeding). Simple navigation (map, compass, landmarks). Lighting a camping stove with an adult. Treating water with a filter. Reading a weather warning. Their own torch and radio.
13–17First aid course (St John Ambulance and Red Cross run youth courses). Map and compass on the hills. Building a tarp shelter. Cooking for the family on a stove. Radio use (PMR446; the Foundation licence has no minimum age). Responsibility for younger siblings in the plan.

Knives and children

Under-18s can't buy knives in the UK. A supervised bushcraft knife used at camp is fine. Carrying one in public without good reason is an offence at any age (see Bug-Out Bag).

3. Children's Grab Bags

What goes in each child's bag:

  • A small backpack they carry themselves: no more than 10% of their body weight for younger children.
  • Water bottle, snacks (familiar favourites), a spare set of clothes, waterproofs, hat and gloves.
  • A torch, a whistle, and a contact card (names, numbers, meeting points; a photo of the family helps if they're separated).
  • A little cash in a zipped pocket (older children), plus a charged power bank in an adult's bag.
  • A comfort item (soft toy, small game, book, cards). This matters more than anything else in the bag.
  • Prescriptions or medicines, held by an adult.

Babies (0–2):

  • Ready-to-feed formula (no water or sterilising needed) and disposable bottles.
  • Nappies (6–8 a day), wipes, nappy sacks.
  • A sling or carrier (better than a pushchair on rough ground), and warm layers.
  • Breastfeeding is the safest food supply in an emergency. Support and protect it.

Use the Bag Builder to build a bag sized for your household.

4. Babies & Power Cuts

  • Sterilising without power:
    • boil bottles and teats for 10 minutes on a camping stove (outdoors);
    • or use cold-water sterilising tablets;
    • or use single-use pre-sterilised bottles.
  • Warmth: babies lose heat fast. Use layers plus a hat, and share a bed only where safe sleeping guidance allows. A baby sleeping bag (grobag) is safer than loose blankets.
  • Heat: keep them in the shade, offer extra feeds, and never leave them in a car.

5. Older & Disabled Relatives

  • Priority Services Register (free) with the energy and water companies: priority updates, help in outages, and password schemes.
  • Medication: an emergency supply where possible (ask the GP or pharmacist), a list of medicines and doses, and spare glasses and hearing-aid batteries.
  • Powered equipment (oxygen concentrators, stairlifts, CPAP, powered wheelchairs): plan a battery or generator backup and tell your network operator. See the Solar Sizer.
  • Mobility: an evacuation plan for stairs, and a neighbour who will check on them.
  • Dementia: an ID bracelet, a recent photo, and a familiar routine. Crises cause confusion and wandering.

6. Keeping Spirits Up

Routine is medicine. Keep regular meals, bedtimes and simple "jobs" for children (water monitor, torch keeper, radio reporter).

Talk honestly at their level:

  • "The power is off for a while; we have a plan; we're together."
  • Limit their exposure to frightening news.

Have games in the kit:

  • cards, dice, paper and pencils;
  • a Morse-code challenge (see the Morse Trainer);
  • a star-spotting night (see Navigation).

Look after the adults too: sleep in shifts, eat properly, and talk to each other.

7. Drills That Don't Scare Anyone

  • "Lights Out" evening: an evening with no electricity. Cook on the camping stove, play games by lantern, and find out which batteries are flat.
  • Fire drill: twice a year, from bedrooms at night. Check the smoke alarms on the same day.
  • Grab-bag race: everyone fetches their own bag. Check sizes and swap outgrown clothes, every season.
  • Family walk with a map: let the kids navigate to a café with an OS map.
  • A camping trip is the best prep of all: shelter, cooking, water, warmth and fun.

8. Collapse Collapse Protocol: Families

🔴
COLLAPSE PROTOCOL: Disclaimer

This covers long-term breakdown only.

  • Families with young children can't move fast or far, so plan a destination close enough to walk to (a relative's house within 20–30 miles), and get there early.
  • Children need more: more calories per kilogram, more warmth, more water in heat, and more reassurance. Prioritise them in rationing, and make sure the adults still eat enough to stay capable.
  • Education continues. Reading, maths and practical skills keep children's minds busy and build the next generation's competence. Pack a few books.
  • Join a group. Childcare is easier shared. A community with nurses, teachers and grandparents is the strongest unit there is.
