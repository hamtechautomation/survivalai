1. Why Prepare, and for What

The UK government asks every household to be able to manage for 72 hours if power, water, phones or food supplies are cut off. That is not because a disaster is expected. Help is stretched thin in the first days of any big event, and the people who can look after themselves let it reach those who can’t.

Most prepping is for ordinary, likely events, not the end of the world. The UK’s National Risk Register lists the things most likely to disrupt daily life: severe storms and flooding, heatwaves and cold snaps, long power cuts, pandemics, cyber attacks on essential services, and supply-chain shortages. Prepare well for those and you are most of the way to coping with the rare and dramatic ones.

Do the boring rungs first

Three days of water saves more lives than any bunker. Most people who get into trouble in UK emergencies had no water, no torch, no medicines and no plan, not a lack of specialist kit.

3. Where You Live Changes Everything

A semi-detached house, a tenth-floor flat, a campervan and a hill farm need different plans. Pick yours for the first five things to do, then read that page.

4. Prepping Myths

MythReality
“Start with a bunker.”Start with water, a torch and a plan. A refuge room in your house is enough for almost every UK emergency.
“Bug out to the woods.”Home is warmer, drier, stocked and known. Staying put is the right call in most events; leave when home itself is dangerous. See Bug-Out vs Bug-In.
“Buy a 25-year food bucket and you’re done.”Store what you already eat and rotate it. Food you don’t like, can’t cook or have forgotten about goes to waste.
“Bury a shipping container.”Their walls aren’t built to hold back soil; buried containers cave in. See why.
“Kit matters most.”Skills and practice matter more. A weekend with the power off teaches more than any shopping list.
“Prepping is selfish.”The prepared household isn’t queueing for help, and has spare to share. Prep with your neighbours.
“It’s expensive.”Ten pounds a week builds two weeks of stores in about three months (§5).

5. Building It on a Budget

The cheapest way to build a store is to buy one extra of the long-life food you already eat each time you shop, and put it at the back of the cupboard. Do that every week and the store builds itself while you rotate it by eating normally. Spend a little each week on kit as well:

6. Your Household Plan

Kit is useless if nobody knows where it is or what to do. Twenty minutes around the kitchen table fixes that. Agree two meeting points (one near home, one outside the area), one out-of-area contact everyone calls, and who collects the children. Then print the plan and put copies on the fridge, in each grab bag and in the car.

7. Practice Weekends

Practising is where you find out what you forgot. Learn the First 24 Hours players first, then live them. Each drill takes a day or a weekend. Afterwards, sit down and write what went wrong, then fix one thing. Tick boxes save as you go.

Drill 1: Lights out (24 hours)

Switch the mains off at the fuse box (leave the fridge and freezer on their own circuit if you can, or keep them closed). Live for a day on your Tier 1 kit.

  • Found torches in the dark, without a phone torch
  • Cooked a hot meal on the camping stove, outdoors or by an open door
  • Heard the news on a battery or wind-up radio
  • Kept phones charged from power banks or solar
  • Kept one room warm (if winter) with blankets, curtains and people
  • Knew how to reset the fuse box afterwards
  • Wrote down three things to fix

Drill 2: Water off (one weekend)

Turn the stopcock off. Drink, cook, wash and flush from your stored water only, and measure how much you use.

  • Found and turned the stopcock (and it wasn’t seized)
  • Measured how many litres the household used each day
  • Flushed the toilet with a bucket of washing-up water
  • Used the bucket toilet kit at least once
  • Treated a litre of rainwater or stream water with the filter
  • Refilled and dated the stored water afterwards

Drill 3: Ten-minute grab

Set a timer for ten minutes. Everyone gets their grab bag, shoes, coat, medicines, phone and charger, and the pets in carriers, then meets at the near meeting point.

  • Everyone out with bags in under ten minutes
  • Medicines and glasses came too
  • Pets caught and in carriers
  • Car keys, house keys, wallet and documents in hand
  • Everyone knew the near and far meeting points

Drill 4: The walk home

On a dry weekend, walk home from work or school with your get-home bag, the long way round, without using your phone’s map. Work out the time with the walk-home calculator first and compare.

  • Walked the whole route with the bag
  • Found a second route avoiding bridges and the city centre
  • Noted where to get water and use a toilet along the way
  • No blisters (or found out which socks and shoes to change)
  • Checked the timing against the calculator

Drill 5: A night in the van or shelter room

Spend a night in the campervan off-grid, or in your refuge room with the door shut. You will learn about ventilation, condensation, light, noise and toilets.

  • Slept warm without mains power
  • Carbon monoxide alarm fitted and tested
  • Checked the air: no stuffiness, headache or condensation dripping by morning
  • Toilet plan worked
  • Battery and water levels checked in the morning against the van calculator
