Country households are often half-prepared already: freezers full, wood stacked, neighbours who can mend things. But help is further away, roads close first, and many rural homes depend on pumps and boilers that stop without power. This page covers water, fuel, heat and food on a rural property, the rules on storing fuel, and how to cope when you’re the help that arrives first.

1. When You’re Cut Off

Snow, flooding and fallen trees cut off rural homes for days at a time. An ambulance may take an hour or more; the power may be off longer than in town, because the lines are long and overhead. Plan for two weeks without outside help as your baseline, not three days.

  • Directions: postcodes often point to the wrong place in the countryside. Write down clear directions to your home for 999 calls (road name, landmarks, the gate colour), and know your map grid reference. Keep it by the phone.
  • Medical: with help far away, first aid matters more. Consider a course beyond the basics, and a kit that can stop serious bleeding. See Medical & First Aid.
  • Phones: mobile signal is patchy and masts lose power. By 31 January 2027 every landline goes over the internet and stops with the router in a power cut, unless it has a battery backup. A battery backup for the router helps; a radio for news is essential. More: Communications.

If your home has a private water supply, your council is responsible for checking it and can test it. Test after floods, and have a way to treat it if the UV lamp is off: boiling, bleach or a filter. Building tanks, rainwater systems and wells: Water Systems.

3. Fuel: How Much You Can Store Legally

Petrol is the most dangerous fuel to keep at home: its vapour is heavier than air and ignites from a spark across a room. In Great Britain, storing it at home is controlled by the Petroleum (Consolidation) Regulations 2014.

4. Heat for a Winter

Oil and LPG boilers, like gas ones, need electricity for their burners and pumps. A rural home’s best insurance is a wood or multi-fuel stove that works with no power at all, plus a winter’s dry wood.

  • Dry wood only: below 20% moisture. Split it, stack it under a roof with open sides, and give hardwood a year or two. A cheap moisture meter settles arguments.
  • How much: a stove heating a house through a UK winter can burn several cubic metres. Work out yours with the Firewood & Heating Estimator, and stay a year ahead.
  • Chimneys: swept at least once a year; a CO alarm in the room with the stove.
  • Cooking on the stove top saves your gas and keeps a kettle hot all day.

5. Backup Power

  • Decide what must run: the borehole pump, the freezer, the boiler’s controls, lights, the router. That list sets the size of what you need.
  • Generator: wired in only through a changeover switch fitted by an electrician (never through a socket). Run it outdoors. Keep fuel for it, within the limits above.
  • Solar with batteries runs the small, essential loads for free once fitted. Rural sites often suit small wind or hydro too: Power Generation.

6. Growing and Keeping Food

  • A vegetable patch and a polytunnel are the biggest step towards a year’s food. Potatoes, beans, squash and kale give the most calories and keep. Planning: Agriculture and the What to Plant This Month tool.
  • Save seed from open-pollinated (not F1 hybrid) varieties, so next year’s crop doesn’t depend on a shop.
  • Preserve the glut: canning, drying, salting, fermenting, root clamps: Food Preservation.
  • A chest freezer is only as good as your power. Can or dry the things you can’t afford to lose.

7. Livestock and the Rules

  • Hens are the easiest start: eggs every day from a few birds and kitchen scraps. Since October 2024, everyone in England and Wales who keeps poultry must register with the Animal and Plant Health Agency, however few birds; Scotland runs its own register.
  • Pigs, sheep, goats and cattle need a County Parish Holding (CPH) number for the land, and animals must be identified and their movements recorded.
  • Stock up for them too: feed, bedding, wormers, and water for two weeks. A cow can drink 50–100 litres a day.
  • Care, breeding and health: Animal Husbandry and Veterinary Care.

8. Community: You’re the Help

In the countryside, the first help after a storm is usually a neighbour with a tractor and a chainsaw. Prepare as a community:

  • Community emergency plans: many parish and community councils have one, listing who has a generator, a 4×4, a chainsaw, medical training, or a hall with a stove. Ask yours; if there isn’t one, offer to start it.
  • Check the vulnerable: isolated, elderly and disabled neighbours are most at risk when roads close.
  • Share skills and tools: one good chainsaw, a log splitter and a generator can serve a hamlet.
  • Long-term: Governance (running a community), Group Dynamics.

9. Rural Security

Isolated property attracts theft of fuel, tools, quad bikes and machinery, and in a long crisis, of food. Locking fuel caps and tank alarms, security lighting, marked tools, and neighbours who notice strangers do most of the work. Don’t advertise your stores. More: Security & Defense.
