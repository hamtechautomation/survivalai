The essentials

Bacteria need warmth, moisture, food and time. Every method below removes at least one of them: drying (moisture), salt and sugar (moisture), acid (pickling), fermentation (acid), smoke and cold (slows growth), or heat plus a sealed jar (bottling). Danger zone: keep chilled food below 8 °C (aim for 5 °C) and hot food above 63 °C. Botulism is the big one. Low-acid food (meat, fish, beans and most vegetables) in sealed jars must be pressure-canned. A boiling water bath is only safe for acidic foods (most fruit, pickles, jam). If in doubt, throw it out. Bulging lids, spurting liquid, off smells or mould on low-acid food: don't taste it.

📚
Use tested recipes

The UK has no official home-canning guidance, so the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning and the US National Center for Home Food Preservation are the reference standards for times and pressures. Follow tested recipes exactly; don't improvise processing times.

2. Drying

  • Dehydrators run at 50–70 °C. A fan oven on its lowest setting with the door ajar works. In summer, sun and airflow dry thin slices of fruit and herbs under muslin (protect them from flies).
  • Slice thin and even (3–6 mm). Dry until leathery or brittle, with no moisture when torn.
  • Condition before storing: pack loosely in a jar for a week and shake it daily. If you see condensation, dry it more.
  • Jerky safety: use lean meat and trim the fat (fat goes rancid). Heat the meat to 71 °C (before or after drying) to kill pathogens; drying alone at low temperatures isn't enough. Marinate in salt and spices.
  • Fish: split, salt, and dry in a breezy, shaded spot, or smoke it.

3. Salting & Curing

  • Dry salt: bury cleaned fish or pork in salt, with weights on top, in a cool place (under 10 °C). Liquid is drawn out and the salt soaks in. Classic examples: salt cod, bacon.
  • Brine: for hams and fish before smoking. Weigh the salt and follow the recipe's percentage; strengths vary widely by recipe (light brines around 5–10%, strong curing brines much higher). The old "a fresh egg floats" test is only a rough check, so don't rely on it for safety.
  • Curing salts containing nitrite stop botulism in cured meats and give the pink colour. They're toxic if overused: measure exactly per the recipe and keep them away from table salt.
  • Desalt heavily salted food by soaking it in several changes of water before cooking.

4. Smoking

  • Hot smoking (60–90 °C+): cooks and smokes together. Mackerel, trout and chicken keep a few days chilled, or longer if well dried.
  • Cold smoking (under 30 °C): adds flavour and dries the surface but doesn't cook. Only cured or salted food should be cold smoked (salmon, bacon, cheese).
  • Wood: hardwoods (oak, beech, ash, apple, cherry). Never use softwoods (resinous), treated or painted timber.
  • A simple smokehouse: a fire pit downhill, an earth-covered flue trench 3–5 m long, and a box or barrel at the top with racks. The trench cools the smoke for cold smoking.

5. Pickling & Fermenting

  • Vinegar pickles: use vinegar of at least 5% acidity. Never dilute it beyond the recipe. Pack the veg, pour over hot vinegar, seal, and leave 2–4 weeks.
  • Sauerkraut:
    1. Shred cabbage and weigh it. Add 2–2.5% of its weight in salt (20–25 g per kg).
    2. Massage until it's wet.
    3. Pack it hard into a jar or crock under its own brine, with a weight on top.
    4. Ferment 2–6 weeks at 15–20 °C, then keep it cool.
  • Mould on the surface: a thin white film (kahm yeast) can be skimmed off. Fuzzy or coloured mould means throw the batch out.
  • Garlic, herbs or chillies in oil are a botulism risk. Oil cuts off air, and these low-acid ingredients can carry spores. Make small amounts, keep them in the fridge and use them within 4 days, or freeze them. Never leave them at room temperature.

6. Jams & Sugar

  • Sugar locks up water and stops microbes. Traditional jams are roughly 60% sugar.
  • Test the set on a cold plate (it wrinkles). Pour into sterilised hot jars and seal at once.
  • Low-sugar jams must be refrigerated or water-bath processed.
  • Hedgerow staples: blackberry and apple, damson, rosehip syrup (strain out the irritant hairs through muslin), elderberry (always cooked).

7. Bottling (Canning)

Rules:

  • Use tested recipes for times and headspace.
  • Adjust for altitude (relevant above about 300 m).
  • Check the seals after 24 hours (the lid stays down).
  • Label and date every jar.
  • Never oven-can, open-kettle can, or water-bath low-acid food.
🚨
Botulism

Signs begin hours to days after eating: blurred or double vision, drooping eyelids, slurred speech, difficulty swallowing, then paralysis spreading downward. Call 999. Antitoxin is needed fast. Botulinum toxin has no smell or taste, which is why the rules above are absolute.

8. Root Stores & Clamps

  • Potatoes: cure for 1–2 weeks after lifting, then store dark (light turns them green and toxic), cool (5–10 °C), humid and frost-free, in hessian or paper sacks.
  • Clamp (traditional British method):
    1. Lay a bed of straw on a well-drained spot.
    2. Pile the roots in a ridge and cover with 15–20 cm of straw.
    3. Cover that with 15 cm of soil, leaving straw "chimneys" for ventilation and a ditch around the edge.
  • Carrots and beetroot: twist the tops off and layer in boxes of damp sand.
  • Apples: wrap individually in paper and store on slatted shelves, kept apart from potatoes. Check monthly: "one bad apple…"
  • Onions and garlic: dry well, then hang in plaits or nets somewhere airy.
  • Squash: cure in the sun, then store at room temperature. Keeps 3–6 months.

9. Storing Dry Goods Long-Term

  • Rice, oats, wheat, beans, pasta: in Mylar bags with oxygen absorbers inside food-grade buckets, these keep 10–25 years (white rice and wheat the longest; brown rice and oats less, because of the oils).
  • Keep them cool, dark and dry, and off the floor. Watch for weevils (a few days in the freezer kills eggs before packing). Keep rodents out with metal bins or buckets.
  • Rotate: "store what you eat, eat what you store". See the Stockpile Calculator.

Eggs, cheese & pemmican

  • Eggs: a cool, dark place keeps fresh eggs for weeks. Unwashed eggs from your own hens keep longest, because washing removes the protective bloom. Traditional long-keeping methods (coating in fat or wax, or "water glassing" in a lime solution) date from before modern food safety. Throw out any egg that smells off or floats, and cook preserved eggs thoroughly.
  • Pemmican: dried lean meat pounded to a powder and mixed about 1:1 by weight with rendered fat (and sometimes dried berries). It is dense and keeps for months if the meat is completely dry, the fat is pure, and it's stored cool. If it smells rancid, don't eat it.
  • Hard cheese waxed or kept in a cool cellar keeps for months. Cut mould away generously from hard cheese only (at least 2.5 cm); throw out soft cheese with mould.

10. Power-Cut Rules for the Freezer & Fridge

  • A full freezer stays frozen for about 48 hours if you keep the door shut (24 hours half full). A fridge keeps food cold for about 4 hours.
  • Food that still has ice crystals (or is at 4 °C or below) can be refrozen.
  • Thawed meat, fish and dairy that is still cold: cook and eat it, or cook and refreeze.
  • Chilled food that has been above 8 °C for more than 4 hours: throw it out (the UK Food Standards Agency rule; the US rule is stricter, 2 hours above about 5 °C). Don't rely on smell or taste. A fridge thermometer, or a coin on a frozen cup of water in the freezer, tells you whether it thawed while you were away.
  • Eat in this order: fridge first, then freezer, then the store cupboard.

11. Collapse Collapse Protocol

🔴
COLLAPSE PROTOCOL: Disclaimer

This covers long-term grid-down conditions. Normal food hygiene and foraging law still apply in normal times.

  • Salt is strategic. Store 25 kg+ per household (it's cheap and keeps forever). Sea salt can be made by boiling seawater down, which takes a lot of fuel. Settle and filter the water first.
  • A smokehouse and drying racks turn hunting and fishing gluts into winter food. A community smokehouse serves a whole village.
  • Pressure canners, spare gaskets, jars and thousands of lids are high-value stores and trade goods.
  • Ice houses and cold cellars: before refrigeration, estates packed winter ice in straw-lined pits. A north-facing cellar keeps 5–10 °C year-round.
  • Lard and dripping: rendered fat seals cooked meat in jars (confit). It's traditional, but it needs cool storage and careful hygiene.

Sources: Food Standards Agency (chilling and the danger zone); USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning (2015 rev.) and the National Center for Home Food Preservation; NHS (botulism); RHS (storing crops); standard curing and smoking practice. Keeping times are typical, not guarantees.
