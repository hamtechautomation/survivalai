The essentials

Call 999/112 first (or get someone else to). Say where you are with a grid reference, and use speakerphone so you can work while the call handler coaches you. Order of priorities: Danger → Response → Shout for help → Airway → Breathing → Circulation (CPR + defibrillator). Massive bleeding comes before everything. CPR: 30 hard, fast chest compressions (5–6 cm deep, 100–120 a minute) then 2 rescue breaths, or hands-only CPR if you can't or won't give breaths. Send someone for a defibrillator; the 999 call handler can tell you where the nearest one is. Get trained. A one-day course (St John Ambulance, British Red Cross) or a 16-hour outdoor first aid course is the best prep you can buy. This page supports training; it doesn't replace it.

Medical disclaimer

General first aid information for the UK, current to Nov 2026 (Resuscitation Council UK, NHS and first-aid charity guidance). It is not medical advice. Always call 999 for emergencies and follow the call handler's instructions.

1. The Primary Survey (DRSABC)

StepDoWhy
DangerStop. Check for traffic, rockfall, water, fire, electricity, fumes. Don't become a second casualty.A dead rescuer saves no one
Response"Can you hear me? Open your eyes." Tap the shoulders.Conscious, or not?
ShoutShout for help. Call 999 on speaker; send someone for a defibrillator.Help takes time on the hills
AirwayHead tilt, chin lift.The tongue blocks the airway when unconscious
BreathingLook, listen and feel for up to 10 seconds. Occasional gasps are not normal breathing.Not breathing normally → CPR now
CirculationNo normal breathing: CPR. Breathing: check for and control bleeding, then the recovery position.

Recovery position (breathing but unresponsive): roll them onto their side, top knee bent to stop them rolling, head tilted back so fluid drains from the mouth. Keep checking their breathing. In the cold, put insulation under them first.

Handing over: write down what happened, the time, what you found, what you did, and any changes. Pass it to the paramedics or rescue team, or give it to the 999 call handler. A notebook in the kit is there for this.

Catastrophic bleeding comes first. If blood is spurting or pooling fast, deal with it before the airway (some courses call this the ABC approach).

2. CPR & Choking

CPR (adult):

  1. Put the heel of your hand in the centre of the chest, with the other hand on top.
  2. Push down 5–6 cm at 100–120 a minute, the tempo of "Stayin' Alive".
  3. Give 30 compressions, then 2 breaths. Or keep doing compressions only.
  4. Use the defibrillator as soon as it arrives: switch it on and follow the voice.
  5. Swap compressors every 2 minutes if you can. Don't stop until help takes over, the person starts breathing normally, or you're exhausted.

Children: start with 5 rescue breaths, then 30:2. Press about a third of the depth of the chest, using one hand (or two fingers for a baby).

Choking (adult):

  1. "Are you choking?" Encourage them to cough.
  2. Up to 5 back blows between the shoulder blades.
  3. Up to 5 abdominal thrusts.
  4. Repeat. If they become unresponsive, call 999 and start CPR.

Babies under 1: use back blows and chest thrusts, never abdominal thrusts.

Drowning: once they're out of the water and not breathing normally, give 5 rescue breaths first, then 30:2. Their heart usually stops because they ran out of oxygen, so the breaths matter. Expect vomiting; turn their head to clear it. Anyone pulled from the water needs hospital checks, even if they seem fine. Don't go in after them: shout "Float!", and reach with something or throw something that floats.

3. Catastrophic Bleeding

  1. Press hard directly on the wound with a dressing or cloth (or your hand). Don't keep lifting it to look.
  2. Pack deep wounds: stuff gauze or a haemostatic dressing into the wound cavity, then press hard for at least 3 minutes (10 for plain gauze).
  3. Tourniquet for life-threatening bleeding from an arm or leg that direct pressure can't control: Improvised tourniquets (a belt with a stick) often fail, so a purpose-made one (CAT- or SOF-T-type) is worth carrying.
    • put it 5–7 cm above the wound (not over a joint);
    • tighten until the bleeding stops;
    • note the time (write it on the casualty);
    • never loosen it once applied.
  4. Lie them down, keep them warm and watch for shock.

4. Shock

Signs:

  • pale, cold, clammy skin;
  • fast, weak pulse;
  • fast breathing;
  • thirst, confusion, then drowsiness.

Do:

  • lie them down;
  • raise their legs if no leg, pelvis or spinal injury is suspected;
  • keep them warm (insulate them from the ground);
  • reassure them;
  • give nothing to eat or drink;
  • call 999.

5. Hypothermia (the UK's biggest outdoor killer)

StageSignsDo
Mild (core ~35–32 °C)Shivering, clumsy, grumpy, "the umbles" (stumbles, mumbles, fumbles, grumbles)Stop. Shelter. Dry clothes, hat, more insulation underneath them than on top. Warm sweet drinks and food if fully alert. Shared body warmth in a sleeping bag.
Moderate (~32–28 °C)Shivering slows or stops, confused, very drowsyHandle gently. Keep them horizontal and insulate them in a hypothermia wrap (bivvy bag, sleeping bag, foil, outer shell). Warm packs on the chest and armpits, not limbs. Call 999. No food or drink unless fully alert.
Severe (<28 °C)Unconscious, barely breathingCheck breathing for up to 1 minute. CPR if not breathing. Rough handling can stop the heart. Evacuation is a job for rescuers.

Don't:

  • rub their limbs;
  • give alcohol;
  • put them in a hot bath;
  • give up on a cold casualty. "Not dead until warm and dead."

Cold-water immersion: float for 60–90 seconds to ride out cold-shock gasping ("Float to Live", RNLI), then get out or signal.

6. Heat Illness

Heat exhaustion:

  • Signs: heavy sweating, headache, dizziness, nausea, pale clammy skin.
  • Do: cool them in shade, lying down, and give sips of water or rehydration drinks. If they're not improving within 30 minutes, treat it as heatstroke.

Heatstroke:

  • Signs: confusion, hot skin, possibly not sweating, temperature 40 °C+.
  • Do: call 999 and cool them aggressively with water and fanning.

More in Fire & Warmth: Heatwaves.

7. Fractures, Sprains & Improvised Splints

  • Support the injury in the position you find it. Splint the joints above and below a broken bone.
  • Improvise splints from: a rolled sleeping mat, walking poles, sticks padded with clothing, a rolled-up newspaper or magazine, a SAM splint.
  • Arm: use a sling (triangular bandage, jacket, buff or pinned sleeve).
  • Leg: tie it to the good leg with padding between them (at the thighs, knees and ankles, avoiding the injury).
  • Check the hand or foot beyond the injury before and after splinting: warm, pink and able to feel touch. If it's cold or numb, loosen the splint.
  • Open fractures: cover the wound with a clean dressing and control bleeding around the bone, not on it.
  • Suspected neck or back injury (fall from height, diving, head injury): keep them still. If you must move them to save their life, keep the head, neck and body in line.
  • Moving a casualty: don't unless you must (danger, or no rescue coming). For a short distance use a two-person seat carry, or drag them on a blanket or tarp. An improvised stretcher is two poles through the sleeves of two or three buttoned jackets, or a rolled tarp around poles. Test it with an uninjured person first. Carrying someone over rough ground takes at least 6 people taking turns.
  • Sprains: rest, ice (wrapped) or cold water, compression, elevation. Strap ankles for walking out only if you must.

8. Wounds, Burns & Blisters

  • Wounds:
    • Irrigate with plenty of clean (drinkable) water: at least a litre, squirted with force. This is the single best way to prevent infection.
    • Close small clean cuts with wound-closure strips.
    • Don't close dirty, deep, bite or puncture wounds: dress them and get medical care.
  • Burns:
    • Cool running water for 20 minutes (it still helps up to 3 hours later).
    • Remove jewellery.
    • Cover loosely with cling film (laid on, not wrapped) or a clean plastic bag.
    • Don't use ice, butter or creams, and don't burst blisters.
    • Call 999 for large burns, burns to the face, hands, feet or genitals, or electrical or chemical burns.
  • Blisters: prevention is everything. Tape hot spots early. Leave intact blisters alone; drain only large painful ones, at the edge, with a sterilised needle, then dress them.

10. UK Bites & Stings

  • Adder bite:
    • Keep calm and still, and remove rings and watches (the limb swells).
    • Immobilise the limb, call 999 or go to A&E.
    • Don't cut, suck or apply a tourniquet.
    • Fatal bites are extremely rare in the UK, but antivenom may be needed.
  • Ticks:
    • Remove with a tick-removal tool or fine tweezers: grip close to the skin and pull slowly and steadily. Don't squeeze, burn or smear the tick.
    • Watch for a spreading "bullseye" rash or flu-like illness for up to 3 months, and see a GP (Lyme disease).
  • Wasp or bee stings: scrape bee stings off. Treat as anaphylaxis if the person reacts badly.

11. First Aid Kit Tiers

Tier 0: Pocket/EDC (£15)

  • Plasters, 2 wound dressings, tape, wipes, gloves
  • Painkillers, antihistamine, your own medication
  • Tick remover, blister plasters

Tier 1: Day pack / car (£60)

  • Everything above, plus: large trauma dressing ×2, triangular bandage ×2, crepe bandage
  • Wound-closure strips, saline pods (irrigation), burn gel or cling film, foil blanket
  • SAM splint, shears, tweezers, notebook and pencil (times, observations)

Tier 2: Home / group (£150)

  • Everything above, plus: tourniquet (and training), haemostatic gauze, chest seals
  • Group shelter or bivvy bag (hypothermia wrap), pocket mask for CPR
  • Rehydration salts, aspirin 300 mg, loperamide, a thermometer, eye wash

Tier 3: Long-term / retreat (£400+)

  • Bulk dressings, gloves and saline; a field reference book
  • Pulse oximeter, blood-pressure cuff, splints of every size
  • Dental first aid kit (temporary filling, clove oil)
  • A spare supply of essential personal medicines (talk to your GP and pharmacist about how to build one safely)

12. Collapse Collapse Protocol: When Help Isn't Coming

🔴
COLLAPSE PROTOCOL: Disclaimer

This applies only when emergency services and hospitals are genuinely unavailable for a long period. Untrained procedures can kill. Never take or give prescription-only medicines meant for someone else.

  • Prevention is the whole game: clean water, handwashing, safe food, safe toilets, care with tools and fire. Most collapse deaths in history came from infection and dehydration, not violence.
  • Wound infection:
    • Signs: spreading redness, heat, swelling, pus, red streaks up the limb, fever.
    • Do: keep wounds open and clean, irrigate and change dressings daily, and isolate infected people's waste and dressings.
  • Diarrhoea: the big killer. Use oral rehydration: 1 litre of safe water + 6 level teaspoons of sugar + ½ level teaspoon of salt (WHO home recipe). Sip constantly.
  • Prolonged care: keep a written log (pulse, breathing, temperature, urine, pain, every few hours) and keep casualties warm, fed, hydrated, turned and clean.
  • Skills to seek out now: outdoor first aid (16 h), then prolonged field-care or expedition medicine courses. Community first responder schemes are another route. Paramedics, nurses, midwives, dentists and vets are the most valuable members of any group.
