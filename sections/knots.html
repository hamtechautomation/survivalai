Knots & Rope

Six knots do nine-tenths of the work: a loop that won’t slip, a hitch to a post, a line you can tighten, a join for two ropes, a way to haul a load down hard, and a binding for bundles. Learn these six properly, with what each is for and how each fails, before you learn a seventh.

1. Which Knot?

Pick the job, not the knot. Each one below is the simplest dependable answer to one job, and each has a way of failing that you should know before you rely on it.

A line you can tightenTaut-line hitchTent and tarp guy lines, a washing line. Join two ropesSheet bendDifferent sizes too; or a rope to a tarp corner. Lash a load down hardTrucker’s hitchCart and roof loads, a ridge line pulled tight. Tie a bundle or bandageReef knotLies flat. Never for joining ropes under load.
⚠️
A knot is not finished until it is dressed and set. Dressed means the strands lie neatly with no stray crossings; set means every strand has been pulled snug. Most knots that fail looked right and were never pulled tight. Nothing on this page is enough, on its own, to trust a life to: climbing and rescue knots need a rated rope and someone to check your work in person.

2. Rope Words

Four shapes make every knot. The step-by-step instructions below use these words and no others.

3. Bowline the loop that won’t slip

A fixed loop at the end of a rope. It does not tighten on what it holds, and however hard it has been pulled it unties easily afterwards.

Use it forTying to a tree, post or ring; a bucket handle; a tarp corner loop; a loop to haul someone a short way.
Don’t trust it whenIt will be shaken slack and reloaded; the rope is stiff or slick; a life depends on it.
To untiePush the collar forward off the standing part (“break its back”); it loosens at once.

How it fails

  • Shaken loose. A bowline that is loaded, slackened and flapped about can work itself undone, most of all in stiff or slippery rope. Leave a long tail, and for anything that matters tie the tail round the loop’s near leg with an overhand knot.
  • Cannot be untied or adjusted under load. Take the weight off first.
  • Pulled sideways. It is made for a straight pull on the standing part. Loaded across the loop, or with the loop held wide open over a large object, it can distort and spill.

4. Clove Hitch fast tie to a post

Two turns round a post, held by their own crossing. Quick to tie, quick to adjust, and the usual start and finish of a lashing.

Use it forStarting and finishing lashings; hanging gear from a pole; a quick temporary tie to a rail or stake.
Don’t trust it whenThe pull swings sideways; the post is smooth, square or free to turn; you are leaving it unattended.
To untiePush the two ends towards each other and the turns open.

How it fails

  • It rolls. If the load swings from side to side, or the post can turn, the hitch walks round and comes undone.
  • It slips on smooth or tapered posts and on square timber, and under a load that comes and goes.
  • Fix: finish with one or two half hitches round the standing part, or use a round turn and two half hitches instead for anything left unattended, including animals.

5. Taut-Line Hitch a line you can tighten

An adjustable loop. It slides along its own standing line when you push it and grips when the line pulls on it, so a guy line can be tightened without retying.

Use it forTent and tarp guy lines; a washing line; any line that needs re-tensioning as it stretches.
Don’t trust it whenThe cord is slick or stiff; the load is heavy; the line will flap slack in the wind.
To untieSlacken the line and pull the end back out.

How it fails

  • Slips in slick, stiff or very thin cord. Add a third turn inside the loop. If it still creeps, the cord is wrong for the job.
  • Loosens when the line goes slack and flaps, as on a tarp in gusty wind. Check guy lines at dusk and after rain.
  • Not for heavy or critical loads. It is a tensioner for lines, not an anchor.

6. Sheet Bend joins two ropes

The knot for joining two ropes, and the one to use when they differ in thickness or stiffness. It also ties a rope to the corner of a tarp or blanket: fold the corner over to make the bight.

Use it forJoining two ropes, especially of different sizes; tying a rope to cloth; lengthening a line.
Don’t trust it whenIt will hang slack and be shaken; the ropes are very slick (use the double version).
To untiePush the thin rope’s loop back over the bight.

How it fails

  • Works loose when slack. It holds under steady pull; shaken about unloaded, it can loosen. Check it before you load it again.
  • Bight in the wrong rope. The bight goes in the thicker or stiffer rope. The other way round, the thin rope cannot grip and pulls out.
  • Short ends on opposite sides slips sooner. Retie it.

7. Trucker’s Hitch pulls a load down hard

Not one knot but a small machine: a loop in the line used as a pulley, so one person can strap a load down far tighter than by pulling alone.

Use it forLashing loads to a cart, sledge or roof; pulling a ridge line or a fence wire tight.
Don’t trust it whenThe cord is thin enough to snap, or the load can be crushed; the same spot on the rope is used every time.
To untiePull out the half hitches and the tension is released at once. Stand clear of the load.

How it fails

  • Over-tightening. It is easy to crush a load, bend a rail or snap thin cord. Pull until the load does not shift, then stop.
  • The loop jams or wears. See the note above. Cut out and discard any section that has gone flat, stiff or glazed.
  • Loads settle. Check and re-tension after the first few minutes of travel and at every stop.

8. Reef Knot binds a bundle

A binding knot: it ties the two ends of one cord round something. It lies flat, which is why it is used for bandages and slings, and it unties with one pull.

Use it forBandages and slings; tying up a bundle or a rolled tarp; parcels; shoelaces.
Don’t trust it whenIt joins two ropes under load; the two ends differ in size or stiffness.
To untiePull one end back across the knot until it flips, then slide it off.

How it fails

⛔
Never join two ropes with a reef knot if they will take a load. Pull on one end and it flips into a shape that slides straight off the other rope. Ropes of different thickness or slipperiness make it worse. To join ropes, use a sheet bend.
  • Granny by mistake. Both half knots tied the same way. It slips, then jams so it cannot be undone.
  • Needs something to press against. Tied round a bundle or a limb it holds; hanging in mid-air between two ropes it does not.

9. Rope Strength & Care

Rule of thumbWhy
A knot costs a third to a half of the rope’s strength.Rope breaks where it bends sharply, which is inside or just beside the knot. Wide curves are stronger than tight ones.
Work a rope at no more than a fifth of its breaking strength, and a tenth where a failure could hurt someone.Shock loads, wear, knots and age all eat the margin. A dropped load can pull several times its own weight.
Keep rope off sharp edges.An edge under load cuts rope fast. Pad it with cloth, bark or a split length of hose.
Seal the ends.Melt synthetic rope; bind natural-fibre rope with twine or tape (a whipping). An unsealed end unlays itself and the rope is ruined from the end inwards.
Dry it, shade it, hang it.Natural fibre rots if stored damp. Sunlight weakens synthetics over months. Coil it loosely and hang it off the ground, away from fuel, battery acid and rodents.
Retire it if it has cuts, flat or stiff sections, a glazed or melted patch, or powder inside when you twist it open.Damage inside a rope does not show until it breaks. Downgrade old rope to light jobs; never to lifting.

Practise each knot until you can tie it behind your back, then tie it in the dark and with cold, wet hands. A knot you have to think about is one you will get wrong when it matters.
