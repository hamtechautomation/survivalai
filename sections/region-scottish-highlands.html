The essentials

This is arctic ground in Britain. The UK's lowest temperature (−27.2 °C) was set at Braemar, and its strongest gust (173 mph) at the Cairn Gorm summit on 20 March 1986. Winter on the tops runs from about October to May. Avalanches are real here. The Scottish Avalanche Information Service (sais.gov.uk) forecasts daily from about December to mid-April. Five of its six areas are on this map. Scots law is different. You have a right of responsible access, including wild camping. But snares are banned, every air weapon needs a certificate, mountain hares are fully protected, and no one may fish for salmon on a Sunday. Rescue: call 999 → Police → Mountain Rescue. On the sea lochs and coast, ask for the Coastguard. Flood warnings come from SEPA, not the Environment Agency: Floodline 0345 988 1188.

1. The Lie of the Land

  • Lochaber: Ben Nevis (1,345 m, the highest point in the British Isles), the Aonachs, the Grey Corries and the Mamores, around Fort William.
  • Glen Coe: steep volcanic peaks (Bidean nam Bian, Buachaille Etive Mòr) and the Aonach Eagach ridge above a deep, narrow glen.
  • Rannoch Moor: a high bog plateau of lochans and peat, crossed by the A82 and the railway, with almost no shelter.
  • The Cairngorms: Britain's largest area of high ground. It is a granite plateau above 1,000 m (Ben Macdui 1,309 m, Braeriach 1,296 m, Cairn Gorm 1,245 m) cut by the Lairig Ghru pass.
  • The straths and glens: Strathspey (Aviemore) and its Caledonian pinewoods; Royal Deeside (Braemar, Ballater); the Great Glen (Loch Linnhe to Loch Ness).
  • The west coast: sea lochs and the remote peninsulas of Ardgour and Morvern, across the Corran ferry.

2. Weather Patterns

  • West is wet, east is cold. Atlantic fronts dump rain on Lochaber and Glen Coe. The Cairngorms and Deeside are drier but far colder, with hard frosts and temperature inversions that leave the glen floor colder than the hillside.
  • Wind is the killer on the plateau. On the Cairngorm plateau, gusts strong enough to knock you over are common in winter, and a white-out can remove every feature.
  • Summer is short and unreliable. Snow can fall on the high tops in any month, and snow patches can last all year in the deepest corries.
  • Midges (May to September) are worst on still, damp evenings in the west. Ticks are common in bracken and long grass; check yourself every evening (see Field First Aid).

4. What's Different in Scotland

The rest of this guide is written mostly for England and Wales. In the Highlands, these rules apply:

TopicThe rule in Scotland
Access & campingThe Land Reform (Scotland) Act 2003 gives a right of responsible access to most land and inland water. That includes lightweight wild camping in small numbers, for 2–3 nights in one place, away from buildings and roads. Follow the Scottish Outdoor Access Code. Use a stove, not an open fire.
Stalking seasonRespect estate signs and route advice while deer are being stalked (mainly late summer to winter); check Heading for the Scottish Hills. Access rights don't extend to taking deer or game.
DeerMale deer have had no close season since 21 October 2023. Red, sika and fallow hinds/does are closed 16 Feb–20 Oct, and roe does 1 Apr–20 Oct. You need the landowner's permission and approved firearms and ammunition.
SnaresBanned since 25 November 2024 (Wildlife Management and Muirburn (Scotland) Act 2024).
Mountain haresFully protected since 1 March 2021. They can't be shot for sport; control is by NatureScot licence only.
Air weaponsEvery air weapon needs an Air Weapon Certificate (or permit) from Police Scotland, since 31 December 2016. Visitors need a visitor's permit.
FishingNo rod licence exists in Scotland, but you need permission (a permit) for almost all fresh water. No salmon fishing on Sundays, by law. Sea fishing from the shore needs no permit.
Sunday shootingThere is no statutory ban on shooting game on a Sunday in Scotland, but by custom it isn't done. Wildfowl and waders may not be shot on Sundays or Christmas Day.

6. Bothies & Refuges

Bothies are unlocked, basic shelters (no electricity, water or beds), maintained by volunteers of the Mountain Bothies Association. They're free to use.

  • The rules:
    • Carry out all rubbish.
    • Bring your own fuel and never burn the building or live wood.
    • Leave it as you found it, or better.
    • Expect to share.
  • Popular ones fill up. Corrour in the Lairig Ghru and the Hutchison Memorial Hut are small and often full. Always carry a tent or bivvy bag, and never stake your life on finding space or on finding the building in a white-out.
  • On the map: Corrour, Hutchison, Ryvoan, the Fords of Avon refuge and the Ben Nevis summit shelter (emergencies only). The CIC Hut and Lagangarbh are private club huts, not public shelters.
  • The lesson of 1971: six people from a school party died near the Feith Buidhe on the Cairngorm plateau in a blizzard while trying to reach a high-level refuge. The plateau refuges were later removed. Turn back early; don't push on for a shelter.

8. Floods & Storms

  • Storm Frank (December 2015): the Dee flooded Ballater, where 307 homes and 60 businesses were hit. In January 2016 the river washed away a section of the A93 between Ballater and Braemar.
  • Storm Arwen (November 2021): gales cut power to about 135,000 homes across the north, and some waited about a week to be reconnected. Many Highland homes rely on oil heating and private water supplies that stop when the power does.
  • Warnings: SEPA runs flood warnings in Scotland. Sign up at floodline.sepa.org.uk or call 0345 988 1188. River levels are at waterlevels.sepa.org.uk.

9. Rescue & Local Information

  • 999 → Police → Mountain Rescue. The volunteer teams on this map are Lochaber, Glencoe, Cairngorm and Braemar. Lochaber is one of Scotland's busiest teams.
  • HM Coastguard flies search and rescue helicopters from Inverness Airport for both mountain and sea rescues.
  • Hospitals: the Belford Hospital in Fort William has an emergency department. Raigmore in Inverness is the main hospital for the Highlands. Distances are long: carry a proper first aid kit and know how to use it.
  • emergencySMS: register your phone in advance by texting "register" to 999. Texts can get through when calls can't.
  • Phone signal is absent in many glens and on most of the plateau. Leave a route card with someone (the map's 🥾 Route check prints one).

10. Bugging Out in the Highlands

Pinch points:

  • the A9 and the A82: single routes in and out, closed by snow and crashes;
  • the West Highland Line and the Highland Main Line: single-track railways;
  • the Corran ferry, the only short crossing to Ardgour.

Where to go:

  • Most people head north to "escape". The Highlands can't feed them: there's very little farmland, and food comes up the A9 and A82 by lorry.
  • Long-term base: low, sheltered ground with woods, fresh water and access to the sea: the west-coast sea lochs (food from the shore) or Strathspey and Deeside (pinewood, deer and rivers, but harsh winters).
  • Never base yourself on the plateau, Rannoch Moor or the high corries. Visit them, don't live there.

11. Kit Adjustments for the Highlands

  • Winter (October–May on the tops):
    • an ice axe and crampons, plus training to use them;
    • goggles, a balaclava and mitts;
    • a group shelter or bivvy bag;
    • a flask and spare food.
  • Maps: OS Explorer 392 (Ben Nevis & Fort William), 384 (Glen Coe), OL57 (Cairn Gorm & Aviemore) and OL58 (Braemar), or the Harvey Cairngorms map. Keep them in a waterproof case, and carry a compass you know how to use.
  • Car kit: a shovel, blankets, water, food, a full tank and a power bank. Snow gates can strand you for hours.
  • Midge net, repellent and a tick tool (May–September).
  • A tent or bivvy, even when you plan to use a bothy.
  • Home: for long power cuts, a heating backup (wood stove or gas heater with CO alarm), stored water if you're on a private supply, and solar and batteries.

12. Collapse Collapse Protocol: the Highlands

🔴
COLLAPSE PROTOCOL: Disclaimer

This describes options only in a long-term breakdown of law and supply. Today, deer poaching, taking salmon without permission and setting snares are serious offences in Scotland. Read the site-wide disclaimer.

  • Water is never the problem; food and warmth are. There is very little arable land. Most food comes in by road.
  • Protein: red deer are the region's largest food resource, and there are a great many of them. They need a rifle, skill and a team to recover (see Butchery). The sea lochs give fish and shellfish all year, but biotoxins are still a risk with no testing.
  • Fuel: pine and birch woods; peat was cut by hand for fuel across the Highlands for centuries. It needs a dry summer to cure.
  • Shelter: stone-built bothies, shielings and farm buildings outlast tents. Thick walls and a stove make a Highland winter survivable.
  • Avoid:
    • the plateau and high corries in winter;
    • Rannoch Moor;
    • the main road corridors, where people will funnel.
  • Community: crofting townships, estates and small glens have deep self-reliance skills: stalking, peat-cutting, boats and livestock. Bring a trade.
