The Prepped Home

Most emergencies are spent at home, and a house is the best shelter you will ever have, if it is ready. This page walks through a typical UK house room by room: what to find, what to store and where, and how to keep warm, fed, clean and informed when the power, water or gas stop.

2. Water at Home

The government asks for 3 litres per person per day for drinking: 9 litres each for 72 hours. Cooking and washing need more, so aim for 5 litres a day each if you can. Work out your household’s numbers with the stockpile planner.

  • Store it: bottled water keeps until its date. Tap water in clean, food-grade containers (25 L jerrycans, or 2 L fizzy-drink bottles washed out) keeps 6 months; date it and refill. Keep it dark and cool, off concrete floors on a board, and away from petrol, paint and chemicals: plastic lets vapours in.
  • Where: ground floor, spread out. 100 litres weighs 100 kg. In a flat, check the floor load.
  • Warning given? If a water outage, storm or freeze is forecast, fill the bath, every pan and bucket. Use bath water for flushing and washing, not drinking.

3. Food When the Power Goes

Keep a store of food that needs no fridge and little or no cooking: tins (meat, fish, beans, soup, fruit), crackers, oatcakes, peanut butter, cereal, UHT milk, dried fruit and nuts, chocolate. And a manual tin opener. How much and how to rotate it: Stockpile & Rotation.

In a power cutRule
FridgeKeep it shut. If it reaches 8 °C or above, throw away chilled food (meat, fish, dairy, leftovers) after 4 hours, unless the power comes back first. A cheap fridge thermometer tells you.
Full freezerAbout 48 hours if unopened; half-full, about 24 (the FSA’s estimates: models vary). Food that still has ice crystals can be refrozen or cooked.
Eat firstFridge food, then freezer food, then your store. Your store is for when the rest has gone.
Winter trickIn a frost, a cool box outside in the shade works as a fridge. Keep it locked from animals.

4. Light and Power

  • Light: a head torch for each person (hands free), and an LED lantern for each room you’ll use. Lithium AA batteries last ten years in a drawer and work in the cold. Candles cause house fires: if you use them, stand them in a jar or on a plate, away from curtains, and never leave them lit.
  • Phones: a 20,000 mAh power bank charges most phones four or five times. A car charger works while the engine is running (outdoors, not in a garage).
  • A portable power station (a big battery with sockets) runs lights, a radio, a router and a laptop for a day or two, recharged by solar. It will not run a kettle, heater or oven for long. Sizing: Off-Grid Power Calculator.
  • Radio: wind-up or battery FM/DAB. BBC local radio carries emergency information. Note your station’s frequency in your plan.
⚠️
Generators kill

A petrol generator makes enough carbon monoxide to kill in minutes. Run it outdoors only, well away from doors, windows and vents: never in a house, garage, shed, porch or tent. Never plug it into the house wiring (a “suicide lead” back through a socket): it can electrocute you, your neighbours, and engineers working on the line. Only a qualified electrician should fit a changeover switch. More: Energy: Generators.

5. Keeping Warm (and Cool)

Most UK homes have gas central heating, and it stops in a power cut: the boiler’s pump, fan and controls run on electricity. In winter, cold is the real danger, especially for babies, older people and anyone ill.

  1. Choose one warm room. Small, few outside walls, ideally ground floor. Everyone lives and sleeps in it.
  2. Seal it: close the door and put a rolled towel at the bottom; curtains shut, a blanket over the window at night; rugs on the floor.
  3. Heat the people, not the room: layers, hats, thick socks, hot water bottles (boiled on the camping stove, outdoors or by an open window), sleeping bags, people sharing body heat.
  4. Eat and drink hot: a flask of hot water lasts all day.
  5. Check on others: neighbours who live alone, and the very old. Hypothermia creeps up indoors. Signs: Medical: Temperature.

Safe heat without mains power: a wood or multi-fuel stove if you have one; a paraffin heater with the room ventilated and a CO alarm. Never bring a barbecue, patio heater, camping stove or gas cartridge heater meant for outdoors into a closed room to heat it.

Heatwaves are now as dangerous: close curtains and windows on the sunny side by day, open everything at night, wet a sheet and hang it in a breeze, and keep drinking.

6. Cooking Without Mains

  • Camping gas stove (butane or butane/propane cartridges) is the simplest. Use it outdoors or by a wide-open door or window, never on a bed or carpet. Butane struggles near freezing: in winter buy propane-mix cartridges and keep them warm in a pocket before use. One 220 g cartridge boils roughly 20 litres.
  • Barbecues and charcoal: outdoors only, and never take a still-warm barbecue into a tent, porch or garage. People die every year from this.
  • No-cook and low-cook food saves fuel: soak oats or pasta in cold water for an hour first; use a rocket stove or solar cooker; a “haybox” (pot wrapped in sleeping bags after boiling) finishes rice and stews without fuel.

7. When the Toilet Stops Working

If the water is off, you can flush a toilet by pouring a bucket of washing-up or bath water into the bowl. But if the water is off for days, or if you are told not to flush because sewers or pumps have failed, you need a bucket toilet.

  1. Two buckets with lids, or a toilet seat that fits a bucket. One for wee, one for poo: kept apart they smell far less, and wee can be diluted and poured on a compost heap or the garden.
  2. Line the poo bucket with a heavy-duty bin bag. After each use cover it with a cup of sawdust, cat litter, soil or shredded paper.
  3. When a bag is a third full, tie it, double-bag it, and store it in a lidded bin outside until collections restart.
  4. Wash hands every time: a water container with a tap, soap and a bowl by the bucket. Diarrhoea spreads fast in a home without running water.

Store one heavy-duty bag per person per day, and about half a kilo of absorbent. Long-term: Sanitation Without Running Water.

8. Medicines and First Aid

  • Repeat prescriptions: order on time, every time, rather than letting them run down to the last day. That keeps a week or two in hand. Some pharmacies can give an emergency supply if you run out. Ask your GP or pharmacist what to do in an emergency for any medicine that is dangerous to stop.
  • Write a list of everyone’s medicines, doses, allergies and conditions. Keep it in your grab bag and your plan.
  • Insulin and fridge medicines: insulin in use can usually be kept at room temperature (below 25 °C) for up to 28 days, but some brands differ: check your leaflet or ask your pharmacist. Keep spares cool, not frozen, in a cool bag in a power cut.
  • Spare glasses, hearing-aid batteries, inhalers, contact lens supplies.
  • First aid kit and a little training. A St John Ambulance or Red Cross course is the single best prep you can do. The guide’s Medical & First Aid section covers the rest.

9. Documents, Money and Contacts

  • Cash: card machines and cash machines need power and networks. Keep enough in small notes and coins for a few days’ food and fuel.
  • Copies of passports, driving licences, birth certificates, insurance, prescriptions and deeds: on paper in a waterproof bag, and scanned to an encrypted USB stick.
  • Numbers on paper: phones die. Family, GP, school, work, insurer, landlord, a neighbour.
  • Insurance: photograph each room and your valuables now; it makes claims after a flood or fire far easier.

10. Children, Older People, Pets

  • Priority Services Register: free, from your electricity network operator and your water company. Older people, disabled people, families with children under five, and anyone who relies on powered medical equipment can register. You get priority warnings and support in a power cut or water outage, and bottled water may be delivered. Register separately with your energy supplier, your electricity network operator and your water company (thepsr.co.uk lists them; 105 finds your network operator). If you rely on a landline, ask your phone provider for a battery backup.
  • Children: know your school’s emergency plan and who is allowed to collect them. Pack a comfort kit (a toy, a book, snacks they like, a torch of their own). Tell them the plan calmly; children cope better when they have a job. More: Psychology: Children.
  • Babies: formula, sterilising (cold-water sterilising tablets), nappies and wipes for two weeks.
  • Pets: two weeks of food and water, medicines, a carrier, lead, vaccination records and a recent photo in case they get lost. Many evacuation centres don’t take pets: plan where they’d go.

12. The Grab Bag

One per person, by the door you leave by. If you must evacuate, this is what you live out of for three days. Keep it light enough to carry (check it with the bag-weight calculator). Ticks are saved.

  • Water: 1–2 L, and a filter bottle or tablets
  • Food: 3 days of no-cook food (bars, nuts, tins with ring pulls)
  • A week of medicines, the medicines list, spare glasses
  • Head torch and spare batteries
  • Power bank, phone cable, small radio
  • Cash in small notes, copies of documents, contact numbers on paper
  • Warm layer, waterproof, hat, gloves, spare socks and underwear
  • Foil blanket or a lightweight sleeping bag
  • First aid basics, hand sanitiser, toilet roll, sanitary products
  • Spare house and car keys
  • Whistle, small knife or multi-tool, duct tape, bin bags
  • Notebook and pencil, a printed copy of your plan
  • For children: comfort toy, snacks, a game. For pets: food, lead, bowl
