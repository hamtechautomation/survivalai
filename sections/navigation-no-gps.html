The essentials

Carry a paper OS map and a baseplate compass, and practise in good weather before you need them in fog. Grid references: "Along the corridor, then up the stairs." Read eastings (left to right) before northings (bottom to top). Time and distance: walking speed is about 4–5 km/h on the flat. Add 1 minute per 10 m of climb (Naismith's rule). No compass? In Britain the sun is due south at about 12:00 GMT / 13:00 BST. At night, find the Pole Star from the Plough. Use the map tools on the British Regions pages for grid references and route timings. Then print the route card.

1. The OS Map

  • 1:25,000 Explorer (orange, "OL" for popular areas): 4 cm = 1 km. Shows field walls and fences, which is the best scale for walking.
  • 1:50,000 Landranger (pink): 2 cm = 1 km. Better for driving and long distances.
  • Grid squares are 1 km × 1 km. Counting squares gives you distance fast.
  • Contour lines:
    • 10 m apart on Explorer maps (5 m on some lowland sheets);
    • close together = steep;
    • V-shapes pointing uphill = a valley;
    • V-shapes pointing downhill = a spur.
  • Colours: green dashed = public footpath, long green dashes = bridleway; open access land has a yellow wash with a brown border.

2. Grid References

  • 4 figures (e.g. SE 60 52) = a 1 km square.
  • 6 figures (SE 603 521) = to 100 m. This is the standard for rescue.
  • 8 figures = to 10 m (a GPS or a careful map reading).
  • Method:
    1. Write the two-letter square (SD, SE, NY…).
    2. Read the easting: the vertical line to the left, plus tenths across.
    3. Read the northing: the horizontal line below, plus tenths up.
  • Your phone's OS Locate app gives a grid reference from GPS with no signal needed. So does the map on our region pages.

3. Compass Skills

Parts of a baseplate compass:

  • the direction-of-travel arrow;
  • the rotating housing with orienting lines and arrow;
  • the magnetic needle (red end points north).

Taking a bearing from the map:

  1. Put the compass edge along the line from where you are to where you're going, with the direction arrow pointing the right way.
  2. Turn the housing until its orienting lines run parallel to the grid lines and the orienting arrow points to map north (the top).
  3. Magnetic variation: in Great Britain the difference between grid north and magnetic north is currently very small (within about a degree or two of zero; check the note in the map margin). For walking it can usually be ignored. Elsewhere in the world, adjust it.
  4. Hold the compass flat in front of you and turn your body until the red needle sits inside the orienting arrow ("red in the shed"). Walk where the direction arrow points.

Walking a bearing in fog:

  • Pick an object on the line (a rock or tuft) and walk to it, then pick the next.
  • With a partner, send them ahead to the edge of visibility and correct them onto the line.
  • Keep the compass away from phones, metal and magnets; they swing the needle.

4. Distance: Pacing & Timing

  • Pacing: count double paces (every time your left foot lands) over 100 m on flat ground. That's typically 60–70. Add more on rough ground or uphill. Use toggles or knots on a cord to count each 100 m.
  • Timing: at 4 km/h, 100 m takes 1.5 minutes. At 5 km/h, it takes 1.2 minutes. Add 1 minute per 10 m of ascent.
  • Naismith's rule: 1 hour per 5 km, plus 1 hour per 600 m of climb. Slow it down for loads, children, winter and darkness.

5. Navigation Strategies

  • Handrails: follow linear features (walls, streams, fences, edges) instead of open moor.
  • Catching features: something that will stop you if you overshoot (a road, a lake, a stream junction).
  • Attack points: navigate to something unmistakable near your target, then take a short, careful bearing from it.
  • Aiming off: aim deliberately to one side of a point on a linear feature. When you hit the stream or wall, you know which way to turn.
  • Relocating when lost:
    1. Stop. Don't keep walking.
    2. Think about the last place you were certain of, and the time and direction since then.
    3. Look for features (slope direction, streams, edges).
    4. Take a bearing and walk to a big catching feature.

6. Finding Direction Without a Compass

The sun:

  • In Britain the sun rises roughly east (north-east in summer, south-east in winter) and sets roughly west.
  • At solar noon it's due south: about 12:00 GMT, or 13:00 BST in summer.

Shadow stick (works on any sunny day):

  1. Push a stick upright into level ground. Mark the tip of its shadow with a pebble.
  2. Wait 15–20 minutes, then mark the new tip.
  3. The line from the first mark to the second runs west → east. Stand with the first mark on your left, and you're facing north.

Analogue watch (northern hemisphere):

  • In GMT: point the hour hand at the sun. South lies halfway between the hour hand and 12.
  • In BST: use 1 o'clock instead of 12.
  • It's rough (±15°), and worse in summer when the sun is high.

The Pole Star (Polaris):

  1. Find the Plough (seven bright stars like a saucepan).
  2. Follow the two "pointer" stars at the end of the pan about 5 times their gap.
  3. The modest star you land on is the Pole Star: true north, within a degree.

Other sky clues:

  • Orion's Belt rises due east and sets due west.
  • A crescent moon: an imaginary line through its horns down to the horizon points roughly south (in the northern hemisphere). Use it only as a rough check.
⚠️
Nature's "signs" lie

Moss "on the north side" of trees, ant hills and tree growth are unreliable in Britain's damp, windy climate. Use them only as a last resort, and cross-check with the sky.

7. Navigating at Night & in Fog

  • Plan it in daylight: write a route card (legs, bearings, distances, timings, escape routes).
  • Head torch on low or red, to keep your night vision. Use a compass with luminous markings.
  • Shorter legs, more careful pacing, and catching features behind every target.
  • In white-out, a rope-length between people and a compass bearing for each leg. Consider staying put in shelter instead.

8. Kit Tiers

Tier 0 (£25):

  • OS Explorer map of your home area (or the free OS Maps app with downloaded maps)
  • Baseplate compass (Silva/Suunto type)
  • Pencil, waterproof notebook

Tier 1 (£75):

  • Laminated maps of your bug-out routes
  • Map case
  • Spare compass
  • Head torch with red mode
  • Pacing beads
  • Romer (grid-reference scale)

Tier 2+ (£250+):

  • Dedicated GPS unit with paper-map backup
  • Altimeter watch
  • Training (Mountain Training "Hill & Moorland Leader" skills courses, orienteering club nights)

9. Collapse Collapse Protocol

🔴
COLLAPSE PROTOCOL: Disclaimer

This covers long-term grid-down conditions. In normal times, follow access and trespass law.

  • GPS can be jammed or switched off in a conflict. Paper maps of your county and routes should be printed and stored (OS maps go out of print, so buy them now).
  • Mark them up:
    • water sources;
    • safe houses;
    • rally points;
    • hazards (flood areas, bridges likely to be blocked);
    • alternative crossings.
  • Old maps (e.g. the free historical maps at the National Library of Scotland) show wells, springs, fords and footpaths that modern maps have dropped.

Sources: Ordnance Survey (map reading guides, magnetic variation notes); Mountain Training; British Orienteering; standard celestial navigation methods.
