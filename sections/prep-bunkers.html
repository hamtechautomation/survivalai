Bunkers & Shelter Rooms

A bunker is the last rung of the ladder, not the first. For storms, chemical releases and even fallout, a well-chosen room in your house does most of what a bunker does, for almost nothing. This page climbs from that refuge room up to a buried, ventilated shelter, and it is honest about air, water, radon, the law and cost, the things that turn a hole in the garden into a death trap or a money pit.

Digging below ground can kill

Trench and excavation collapses kill builders every year: a cubic metre of soil weighs well over a tonne. Never work in an unsupported hole deeper than about 1.2 m, and check for buried cables and pipes before you dig. Anything you will sleep under should be designed by a structural engineer. This page explains the principles; it is not a set of plans.

2. The Refuge Room

Pick it today. The best refuge room is on the ground floor, in the middle of the house, with no windows, or as few as possible: often a hallway, under-stairs cupboard, cloakroom or bathroom. Walls between you and outside slow wind, debris, and fallout radiation.

  • Storm: go there when warned; take a torch, radio, phone, shoes, and something to cover your head.
  • Chemical release: the UK advice is Go in, stay in, tune in. Close windows and doors, switch off ventilation fans and heating that draws in outside air, and listen to local radio. Tape plastic over gaps only if told to; sealed rooms run short of air in hours (calculator below).
  • Fallout: the radiation comes from dust settling on roofs and the ground. The middle of a building, low down, and every bit of mass you can add (bookcases, boxes of books, filled water containers, bags of earth against walls) helps. Full method: NBC: Fallout.

A refuge room set up for fallout. Turn it to see where the mass goes: overhead, around a sturdy table, and against the windows.

  • Water: 3 L per person per day for 3 days
  • No-cook food, a tin opener
  • Torch, radio, spare batteries, power bank
  • First aid kit and medicines
  • Bucket toilet, bags, absorbent, wipes
  • Blankets, pillows, sleeping mats
  • Roll of thick plastic sheet, wide tape, scissors
  • Whistle, dust masks, gloves, sturdy shoes
  • Books, cards, something for the children

3. Upgrading a Cellar or Basement

A cellar already has earth around it, the best shielding there is. Upgrading it is the most cost-effective “bunker” in the UK.

  1. Test for radon first (§8). Cellars in some areas collect it.
  2. Fix the damp: drainage outside, and tanking or a cavity drain membrane inside. A dry cellar can store food; a damp one rots it.
  3. Make a second way out: an escape window, a coal chute hatch, or a well opening with a ladder. A cellar with only one stair is a trap if the house above collapses or burns.
  4. Ventilation: an intake and an exhaust at opposite ends, a fan, and a way to pump air by hand (§6).
  5. Shielding for fallout: the floor above is the thin spot. Loading it with mass (filled water containers, bags of earth, books) right above where you’ll sit adds a lot of protection; check the floor can carry it.
  6. Stores, light and a toilet corner by the exhaust end.

Turning a cellar into a habitable room, or digging it deeper, needs building regulations approval, and often planning permission (§9).

4. Buried Shelters

Below ground in the garden, a shelter is protected by the earth over it. The safe ways to do it use something designed to be buried. Turn the model to see how the parts fit:

Your options:

OptionForAgainst
Prefabricated shelter pod (GRP or steel, made as a shelter)Watertight, comes with vents and hatch, designed for a stated earth coverCostly; craned in; check the maker’s burial depth and soil limits
Precast concrete box culvert sectionsBuilt to carry roads over them; very strongVery heavy (crane), joints must be sealed against water
Large corrugated steel or plastic pipeRound shapes carry soil load well; used for culvertsCramped; ends need strong walls; steel needs corrosion protection
Reinforced concrete, built in placeAny size and shapeMust be designed by an engineer; formwork, steel, concrete pours: a major build

Before you dig: the site

  • Groundwater: dig a trial hole in winter, when the water table is highest. A shelter in wet ground floats, leaks or floods. Avoid flood plains and the bottom of slopes.
  • What’s buried there: check utility maps and use a cable detector. Hitting a power cable or gas main kills.
  • Soil: clay holds water and swells; sand runs; rock means a breaker. Each changes the cost.
  • Spoil: a 3 × 2 × 2.5 m hole makes about 15 m³ of loose earth, over 20 tonnes. Where does it go? (Much of it goes back on top as cover.)
  • Trees: roots crack walls and pull water from clay; stay well clear. Some trees are protected by preservation orders.
  • Access: a digger and a crane need a way in.

5. Why Buried Shipping Containers Collapse

The most common prepper mistake. A shipping container looks like a ready-made steel box, but all its strength is in its corner posts and frame, built for stacking, not in its walls. The sides and roof are thin corrugated steel, around 2 mm thick, made to keep rain out. Bury one, and soil pushes on every face:

Containers do make good above-ground stores and workshops, and can be part of a bermed shelter if an engineer designs a separate structure to carry the soil. On their own, under earth, they fail.

6. Air: the Thing That Kills First

People in a sealed room don’t run out of oxygen first. They poison themselves with the carbon dioxide they breathe out, and overheat and drown in their own humidity. Every shelter beyond a few hours needs fresh air pushed through it: in one end, out the other.

Fresh air per personWhenSource
At least 5 m³/h (3 cubic feet a minute)Cold weather: the bare minimum to keep CO₂ downKearny, Nuclear War Survival Skills
About 20 m³/h (12 cfm)Mild weather; still uncomfortable in a crowded shelter in summerKearny
Up to 68 m³/h (40 cfm)Hot, humid weather: size your pump for thisKearny
  • No flames, ever: no stoves, heaters, candles or generators inside. Carbon monoxide kills in a sealed room long before CO₂ does.
  • Filters: for fallout, the dust is the danger, and a good particle (HEPA) filter on the intake stops it. For chemicals you need an activated-carbon NBC filter unit rated for it, and a slight overpressure inside so air leaks out, not in.
  • Heat and damp: each person gives off about 100 W of heat and most of a litre of water a day in breath and sweat. In summer, heat, not CO₂, is the limit, which is why the hot-weather airflow is so much higher.
  • The Kearny Air Pump, a hand-built swinging-flap ventilator, is described in Nuclear War Survival Skills, bundled in the PDF library.

7. Earth Cover and Shielding

Fallout radiation is gamma rays, which pass through things but are weakened by mass. Every material has a halving thickness: the depth that cuts the radiation in half. Ten halvings cut it to about a thousandth, a protection factor of 1,000.

8. Damp, Radon, Exits and Power

Damp

Underground rooms are cold and wet. Water comes in through the walls (keep it out with a membrane, sealed joints and drainage around the outside), and from the people inside (condensation). Fit a sump with a pump, and a hand pump as backup, at the lowest point.

Radon

Radon is a radioactive gas that seeps from the ground, and it is the second biggest cause of lung cancer in the UK after smoking. It collects in cellars and underground rooms, especially in parts of Cornwall, Devon, Somerset, Derbyshire, Northamptonshire, and parts of Wales and Scotland. Check the official UK radon map for your postcode, and test any underground space for three months with a detector from the UK’s radon service before you spend time in it. Ventilation and a sealed floor bring it down.

Getting in and out

  • Two ways out, at opposite ends. Debris, fire or flood can block one.
  • A hatch that opens with weight on it: designed to open inwards, or with a strong lifting mechanism, and lockable from inside.
  • A bend in the entrance, for radiation and blast (above).

Power, light and water

  • Batteries inside, charged from solar panels outside, run LED lights, fans, a radio and phones. Cables come in through sealed glands.
  • Store 3–5 L of water per person per day for at least two weeks (fallout protection time). For 4 people that’s 170–280 L: plan the space.
  • Toilet corner by the exhaust end: bucket toilet, bags, absorbent, and a lid.

9. The Law: Planning and Building Rules

There is no special category for a “bunker” in UK planning law. Councils treat it as a basement, an outbuilding, or engineering works, and the rules vary:

PermissionWhen you need it
Planning permissionUsually for digging a new basement or underground structure, especially if anything shows above ground (a mound, hatch, vents, lightwell). Converting an existing cellar usually doesn’t. Ask your council for pre-application advice first.
Building regulationsAlways for a new basement or habitable underground room: structure, fire escape, ventilation, damp proofing, electrics. Building control inspects as you go.
Party Wall noticeDigging within 3 m of a neighbour’s building (deeper than its foundations), and sometimes within 6 m: you must give notice under the Party Wall etc. Act 1996.
Listed building consent, conservation areas, tree preservation ordersExtra consent if any apply.
Scotland and Northern IrelandSeparate planning and building-standards systems; the same principles apply.

Building without consent risks enforcement action (being told to fill it in) and problems when you sell the house. Done openly, a shelter is just a well-built cellar.

11. Living Underground

  • Keep a day: lights on and off at normal times, meals at set times, a rota for the pump, watch and chores. Routine keeps morale up.
  • Hygiene: hand washing every time, the toilet sealed, rubbish bagged. Illness spreads fast in a small space.
  • Sleep: sleeping mats or bunks; earplugs and eye masks.
  • Children: books, games, drawing, jobs to do. More: Psychology: Children and cabin fever.
  • When to come out: for fallout, follow the radiation readings and official advice: the 7-10 rule.
